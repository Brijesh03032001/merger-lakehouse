# Benchmarks

Local scale benchmark of the real pipeline notebooks, generated by `benchmarks/run_benchmark.py` and rendered by
`benchmarks/render_results.py` from `benchmarks/results/*.json`. Re-run with `make benchmark ROWS=5000000`.

## What this is, and is not

- One laptop: **arm64, 10 cores, 16 GB RAM**, macOS-26.5.2-arm64-arm-64bit.
- Open-source **Spark 4.0.1 and Delta Lake 4.0.1 in local mode** (`local[*]`, 8g driver, 32 shuffle partitions).
- **Not Databricks.** No cluster, no Photon, no cloud storage, no network. Absolute times will differ there; the ratios and the shapes of the curves are the useful part. **Dollar cost per run was not measured.**
- Orders are synthetic, with error rates matched to the project's documented landing zone (about 7% duplicates, 10% bad customer ids, 5% bad quantities, five date formats). They are not production data.
- Single run per row; the micro-batch figures are the median of 3. Expect run-to-run noise of about a second.

## Reading the results

- **Throughput** of the full load was 34,228 to 73,701 rows/s. Going from 5.0M to 20.0M rows (4.0× the data) took 8.6× as long, worse than linear.
- A second identical run of the 5M configuration earlier in development took 79.3 s for the full load (67.8 s recorded here) and measured the batch and Auto Loader daily notebooks at 10.8 s and 10.6 s (14.7 s and 8.7 s here). Treat single timings as good to about ±15%, and small gaps as noise.
- **A 50,000-row day on 5.0M rows of history** took 8.7-14.7 s incrementally, 4.6-7.8× cheaper than reloading everything; the two notebooks differed by 6.0 s in this single run; identical repeat runs at 5M rows differed by about that much, so no speed advantage is claimed either way. The reason to prefer Auto Loader is correctness (exactly once, no failure on an empty day, no type-inference breaks), not speed.
- **A 50,000-row day on 20.0M rows of history** took 10.0-14.1 s incrementally, 41.4-58.4× cheaper than reloading everything; the two notebooks differed by 4.1 s in this single run; identical repeat runs at 5M rows differed by about that much, so no speed advantage is claimed either way. The reason to prefer Auto Loader is correctness (exactly once, no failure on an empty day, no type-inference breaks), not speed.
- **Layout at 5.0M rows.** `OPTIMIZE ... ZORDER` took the selective query from reading 11 files to 3 (a real saving in data scanned). Wall-clock query time went 75 → 81 ms (no measurable change), and micro-batch MERGE time moved by at most 0.9 s (within noise). The date bound on the MERGE condition changed micro-batch time by +0.5 s before and +0.1 s after optimising (within noise, so no benefit is claimed).
- **Layout at 20.0M rows.** `OPTIMIZE ... ZORDER` took the selective query from reading 11 files to 6 (a real saving in data scanned). Wall-clock query time went 99 → 93 ms (no measurable change), and micro-batch MERGE time moved by at most 0.8 s (within noise). The date bound on the MERGE condition changed micro-batch time by +2.4 s before and +1.7 s after optimising (a measurable effect).
- `OPTIMIZE ... ZORDER` reduced the files a selective query reads, but a laptop's local disk hides what that buys on cloud object storage, so no wall-clock speedup is claimed from it. The date bound on the MERGE condition is different: its effect appears only as the table grows, which is what skipping files should do.
- The quality-contract step is the largest cost of the full load and grows faster than the data. The batch notebook evaluates it several times, and serverless compute cannot cache the result. Evaluating it once is the most valuable performance change available here; it was measured but not made, because those notebooks are the ones verified on Databricks Free Edition.
